# Pinecone Pass — Game Brief

**Working title:** Pinecone Pass  
**Format:** Cozy, low-input campground management game for the web  
**Prototype length:** Seven in-game days; approximately 20–35 minutes  
**Core promise:** *Build a place people remember without manually operating every object inside it.*

## Logline

You inherit a worn but beloved campground beside a mountain lake. Each morning, place a handful of visitors, choose a few broad priorities, and then watch the campground run itself. Small stories reveal how your choices affect guests, nearby residents, and the landscape. At the end of the week, the town council decides whether Pinecone Pass should grow—and what it should become.

## The player fantasy

The player is a thoughtful host and steward, not an exhausted clerk. They decide **who goes where, what kind of day the camp will create, and which tradeoffs are acceptable**. Helpers perform the stocking, cleaning, repairs, and setup automatically.

The intended feeling is: “I made only a few decisions, but they produced a story I want to tell.”

## Design pillars

### 1. Every click expresses a preference

No repeated watering, shelf filling, trash collection, gift grinding, or walking between menus. Each morning begins with a sensible suggested plan. The player can accept it in one click or change only the decisions they care about.

### 2. Plan, watch, react

A day has three beats:

1. **Plan:** Assign three visitor parties to four distinct campsites. Pick one store focus, one crew priority, and one shared activity.
2. **Watch:** The day plays out across morning, afternoon, and evening. Visitors move through camp while short narrative moments reveal matches, clashes, and surprises.
3. **Reflect:** Guest postcards and changes to four persistent measures show the consequences. On key days, choose one lasting improvement.

### 3. A living place, not a spreadsheet

The campground map visibly changes through weather, activity, visitor movement, and improvements. Results arrive through postcards and incidents rather than dense dashboards. Numbers support the story; they are not the story.

### 4. Growth creates real tradeoffs

Four resources pull against one another:

- **Guest joy:** Did people have a memorable stay?
- **Town trust:** Does Pinecone Pass benefit and respect its neighbors?
- **Nature health:** Is the landscape better or worse after hosting visitors?
- **Camp funds:** Can the business survive and improve?

There is no hard failure. Weak decisions create complications, funny compromises, and different endings rather than a reload screen.

## Setting and tone

Pinecone Pass sits beside Mirror Lake outside a fictional western mountain town. The tone is warm, observant, gently funny, and grounded in recognizable tourism tensions: traffic, quiet hours, trail wear, accessibility, festival overflow, local business, wildlife, and the difference between welcoming more people and simply becoming busier.

The world should feel handcrafted rather than generically “cozy.” Character details are specific: the bird club wakes before dawn, a child returns with a duck field guide, the local band needs a power hookup, and a travel writer wants the town behind the brochure.

## Prototype content

The seven-day prototype includes:

- 7 authored day scenarios and weather patterns
- 21 visitor parties with overlapping and conflicting needs
- 4 campsites with distinct strengths
- 4 store strategies and 4 crew priorities
- 28 day-specific activity choices
- Noise conflicts between neighboring campsites
- 6 potential campground improvements across 2 build moments
- 4 persistent resources and several council outcomes
- A complete beginning, escalation, and final vote

## Audience and positioning

The target player likes the warmth and progression of *Harvest Moon* or *Animal Crossing*, the small-business/community tension of *Discounty*, and the observational pleasure of compact management games—but is tired of chore loops and high click counts.

The clearest positioning line is:

> **A cozy campground game where you make the plan, then watch the stories happen.**

## Prototype success criteria

The prototype is promising when playtesters:

- understand the daily loop without explanation after Day 1;
- finish a typical plan in under three minutes;
- change the suggested plan for expressive reasons, not because it is obviously broken;
- remember at least two visitor parties by name or personality;
- can recount one emergent story from their week;
- feel tension among guest, town, nature, and financial goals;
- want to begin a second week with different priorities.

## Deliberate exclusions

The prototype does **not** include direct avatar movement, farming, fishing, crafting, romance, combat, multiplayer, procedural dialogue, a freely explorable town, accounts, a backend, or monetization. Those features would obscure the central question: is the low-click **plan → watch → react** loop fun on its own?

## Product risk to test first

The primary risk is that the simulation feels like watching an answer rather than playing a game. The prototype addresses this by making visitor placement, conflicting needs, broad priorities, persistent improvements, and the final council outcome legible. Playtesting should focus on whether players feel genuine ownership of the resulting stories.
